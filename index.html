Problem

Filter life in a domestic RO is estimated, not measured.

The purifier counts litres and days, then turns that into a percentage on the display. The number cannot tell a cartridge that has been passing hard water at low pressure from one that is still clean. It never notices a housing that was refitted without its cartridge, because nothing in the machine checks the water path.

What is measured today is water quality at the tap: TDS in, TDS out. That reading tells you how much dissolved material the membrane is rejecting on the day. It says nothing about the sediment and carbon stages in front of it, which is where loading and refit mistakes actually happen.

  • 3 cartridges in a typical domestic RO
  • 2 sensors that report anything today: TDS in, TDS out
  • 0 measurements of the cartridges themselves

Idea

A pressure tap on both sides of every element in the water path — including the pump.

At a steady flow, the pressure lost across a cartridge is its resistance times the flow. Divide the measured drop by the flow and the reading becomes a property of the cartridge rather than of how much water the household is using that evening. The booster pump's boost gets the same treatment: the tap pair around it reads the motor's health, and a boost sagging below its own baseline is a pump going weak long before the flow dies.

ΔP = k · Q · Rstate

A removed or bypassed cartridge has almost no resistance, so its normalised drop collapses toward zero. A loaded one climbs. The controller compares each stage with its own baseline, recorded when the cartridge was new, and names the stage that left the loop.

Validation plan

A bench rig, a reference gauge, and four states that must be told apart.

Every pressure module is checked against a calibrated differential pressure gauge before it goes near the purifier. Flow is set with a needle valve and confirmed with a graduated cylinder and a stopwatch, so the normalisation is never taken on trust.

  1. New A fresh cartridge set, all three at baseline.
  2. Clogged Sediment stage loaded with test dust to roughly three times baseline.
  3. Cartridge 2 removed Carbon block taken out, housing refitted and sealed.
  4. Cartridge 3 bypassed A valve routes water around the membrane.
  5. Interlock With any water-path fault latched, the dispense valve refuses to open.

Each state is read at 0.25, 0.40 and 0.55 L/min, three repeats, with water temperature logged. Bench-test targets, not results: normalised ΔP repeats within ±4%; removed and bypassed states read below 0.35× baseline; pump boost repeats within ±8% at a given flow; a loaded stage is flagged before flow falls below 0.30 L/min.

Fail-safes

The water path is checked continuously, and the unit acts on what it finds.

Fail-locked dispense.
A stage below 0.35× baseline — a housing refitted without its cartridge, or a bypassed membrane — locks the dispense valve. The tap refuses until the fault clears. In the simulation, press Dispense with a fault latched.
Dry-run guard.
No feed pressure stops the pump before it runs dry, and it resumes on its own when water returns.
Leak shutdown.
Flow with the dispense tap closed closes the inlet solenoid and stops the pump.
Tank-full stop.
The float switch pauses production at full storage; drawing water reopens it automatically.
Pump watch.
Boost that sags below 0.75× its flow-normalised baseline is flagged for service before the flow dies.

On the bench prototype the dispense interlock and the dry-run guard run in firmware on the same controller that reads the sensors; the leak loop closes a solenoid rather than only sounding an alarm.

Build plan

Four weeks from bench rig to a measured four-state result.

  1. Week 1 Bench manifold Plumb one cartridge into a loop with a pump and a needle valve. Tap both sides and check the module against the reference gauge at three flows.
  2. Week 2 Signal Sample at 20 Hz, zero at no-flow, compensate for temperature. Publish normalised ΔP per stage and store the new-cartridge baseline.
  3. Week 3 Integration Fit four modules, the flow sensor and two probes into the purifier shell. Wire the controller and display, then leak-test at 700 kPa.
  4. Week 4 Four-state test Run the validation protocol, log eight hours of household draw, and document every place the model disagrees with the reference.

Honest limits

What this instrument cannot tell you.

TDS is not a safety measure.
It counts dissolved solids. A low reading does not mean the water is safe: pathogens, nitrate and arsenic can pass a membrane without moving the number much. Safety needs microbiological and chemical testing, and this device does not replace it.
Pressure is a proxy, not an assay.
It measures physical loading. A carbon block that has lost its chlorine capacity can still flow perfectly, and this instrument will call it healthy.
Sensors drift.
Piezo modules pick up offset error and temperature sensitivity. The firmware zeroes at no-flow and compensates, but two units will disagree by a few kPa, which is why detection uses a ratio rather than an absolute threshold.
Normalisation has a floor.
Below roughly 0.15 L/min the flow signal is too noisy to trust, so the health reading is only valid while water is running.
This is a concept.
The model on this page is arithmetic, not instrumentation. Nothing here has been built, plumbed or validated yet.
